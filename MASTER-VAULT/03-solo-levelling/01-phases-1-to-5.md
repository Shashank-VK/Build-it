## Phase 1 — Ground Rules, Definitions, and Org Shortlist Lock-In

**Why this phase exists:** every phase after this one depends on shared vocabulary. If "gate" means one thing in Phase 3 and something subtly different by Phase 9, findings across phases stop being comparable, and Phase 13's compression step — which has to line up findings from eleven different phases into one table — quietly breaks. Getting this phase right is disproportionately valuable relative to its size.

**Objective:** lock the confidence-tagging system exactly as defined in the Master Framework (do not loosen it here, even if early searching suggests it'll make later phases harder). Write a precise, one-paragraph working definition for every term that could otherwise be read multiple ways later:

- *Artifact* — a document or output produced at a specific lifecycle stage, with a defined owner and reviewer
- *Gate* — a condition that must be true before work is allowed to proceed to the next stage
- *Decision owner* — the person (or role) whose call it ultimately is
- *Reviewer* — someone whose sign-off is required or requested, but who is not the decision owner
- *Veto authority* — the ability to block a decision outright, distinct from simply reviewing it
- *Reversible ("two-way door") decision* — one that can be undone at acceptable cost if it turns out wrong
- *Irreversible ("one-way door") decision* — one that cannot be cheaply undone
- *DRI (Directly Responsible Individual)* — the named person accountable for an outcome, regardless of who else touches it
- *Escalation* — the defined path for resolving disagreement when normal review doesn't settle it
- *Non-goal* — something explicitly and deliberately excluded from scope, distinct from something simply not yet decided
- *Alternatives considered* — a documented account of rejected approaches and why they were rejected, meant to prevent relitigating
- *Progressive rollout* — releasing a change to a subset of users/traffic before full release, in any of several possible mechanisms (percentage, cohort, geography, time, metric-gated)
- *Blameless postmortem* — an incident review structured to find systemic causes rather than assign individual fault
- *Adversarial review* — a check performed by a party (human or AI) with no stake in the original decision being correct, structured so it can actually catch a real flaw rather than simply agree with the original reasoning

**Also required in this phase:** confirm the six-org shortlist by actually checking, for each candidate, whether a real public *technical* trail exists — not brand reputation. Decide specifically whether the sixth "lean team" slot goes to Basecamp/37signals or Linear, based on which one actually has more usable public writing about its own process (Basecamp's *Shape Up* is a full public methodology book; Linear has extensive public blog writing on "the Linear Method" — compare depth, not fame). Write down, as a standalone line in the findings file, that the AI system's eventual shape remains fully undecided and will not be assumed anywhere before Phase 13.

**Scope lock:** this phase defines terms and confirms the org list. It does not research any company practice yet. If you catch yourself researching, say, Amazon's actual PR/FAQ process here, stop — that's Phase 2.

## Phase 2 — Idea → Evidence → Product Thesis Layer

**Why this phase exists:** most solo developers (and most failed startups) skip straight from "I have an idea" to "I'm building it," skipping the step where the idea is actually pressure-tested against evidence. This phase exists to find out, concretely, what these orgs require to be true before an idea earns real investment — so the solo system can borrow the *discipline* without the *overhead*.

**Objective:** apply the Standard Question Chain plus Lens 1 (Decision Architecture) and Lens 2 (Process Cost) to how each organization validates that an idea is worth building at all, before any design work starts.

**Per-org research angles (starting points — verify, don't assume):**
- **Amazon:** the PR/FAQ (Press Release / FAQ) document is the well-documented starting artifact here. Investigate specifically: who is allowed to write the first draft (is it gated to certain levels/roles, or genuinely open), what the "bar raiser" role checks for specifically at this early stage versus later stages, and whether there's a known minimum evidence bar (customer interviews, a specific metric) before a PR/FAQ is taken to a real review meeting.
- **Google:** investigate how ideas get resourced before a design doc exists — is there an equivalent early-stage document, or does validation happen more informally through manager/lead sponsorship? Look for accounts of Google's historical 20%-time culture and what (if anything) replaced it as a formal idea-validation mechanism.
- **Meta:** investigate how the RFC process's precursor stage works — is there a lighter-weight document before a full RFC, and what triggers moving from "idea being discussed" to "RFC being written"?
- **Netflix:** given the "freedom and responsibility" culture, investigate whether idea validation is centralized at all, or whether individual teams/DRIs are trusted to self-validate — and if so, what (if any) check exists against a bad idea getting resourced purely on one person's conviction.
- **Stripe:** investigate whether Stripe's public engineering writing describes an idea-validation stage distinct from API design itself — Stripe's culture is unusually API-first, so the "idea" stage may be tightly coupled to a proposed API shape from the very start.
- **Basecamp/Linear (whichever was locked in Phase 1):** if Basecamp, investigate the "Shape Up" concept of a "pitch" as the idea-validation artifact and what its actual required sections are. If Linear, investigate how Linear's own public writing describes deciding what to build before formal specs exist.

**Exhaustive question set (apply to each of the six orgs):**
1. What is the first document or artifact that exists once an idea is taken seriously — does it have a specific name, and who is allowed to write its first draft?
2. What counts as sufficient evidence of a real customer problem before that document is taken further — anecdote, a quantified demand signal, a specific metric threshold, executive sponsorship, something else?
3. Who reviews this first document, and is that review a scheduled meeting, an asynchronous comment process, or something else entirely?
4. Who has explicit authority to kill the idea at this stage, and what does "killing" actually look like procedurally — a formal rejection, or does it just quietly stop getting resourced?
5. Is the decision to proceed past this stage treated as reversible or irreversible, and does that classification change who needs to sign off (Lens 1)?
6. What is the actual time and headcount cost of getting an idea through this stage, at the org's real scale (Lens 2)?
7. What happens to a rejected idea — is there a record of why, reusable later, or does the reasoning disappear with the rejection?
8. Is there a known, named, real example (a specific real product) where this org's own accounts describe walking through this exact stage?

**Search strategy guidance:** prioritize first-person accounts from engineers/PMs who worked at each company (Medium/personal blog posts, conference talks, book excerpts) over secondhand "how Amazon works" listicles. For Amazon specifically, *Working Backwards* by Colin Bryar and Bill Carr is a strong primary-adjacent source (written by former Amazon executives) — treat it as close to `[PRIMARY]` but still verify specific claims against a second source where possible.

**Common pitfall in this phase specifically:** conflating a company's stated *values* ("we're customer-obsessed") with an actual *mechanism*. "Customer obsession" is not a process finding; "every PR/FAQ requires a specific customer quote in a specific section, checked by the bar raiser" is. If a search only turns up value statements, that's a signal the mechanism itself isn't well documented for that org — tag accordingly and move on rather than treating the value statement as if it answered the question.

## Phase 3 — Requirements, Scope, and Non-Goals

**Why this phase exists:** "scope creep" is one of the most universally cited killers of solo and small-team projects, and yet almost nobody can point to a *mechanism* that prevents it beyond good intentions. This phase exists to find the actual mechanism, not the value statement about discipline.

**Objective:** Standard chain plus Lens 4 (Change Management). How requirements actually get set, how "non-goals" function as a real enforced section rather than a token gesture, and what concretely happens when someone tries to reintroduce excluded scope.

**Per-org research angles:**
- **Amazon:** the PR/FAQ format is known to include an FAQ section that can be used to explicitly address "why aren't we doing X" — investigate whether this functions as the non-goals mechanism, or whether a separate requirements document does that job.
- **Google:** Google design docs are known to often include an explicit "Non-Goals" heading — investigate how enforced this actually is versus how often it's present but ignored in practice, based on any engineer accounts you can find describing real friction over scope.
- **Meta:** investigate whether Meta's RFC template includes an equivalent section, and whether Meta's fast-iteration culture creates *more* or *less* scope discipline in practice (a legitimate, useful finding either way).
- **Netflix:** given decentralization, investigate whether non-goals are even a company-wide concept, or whether each team defines its own scope discipline independently — and if so, what varies.
- **Stripe:** investigate whether Stripe's API-first culture makes "non-goals" more naturally enforced by the API surface itself (an API that doesn't expose a capability is a de facto non-goal) compared to orgs where non-goals live only in prose.
- **Basecamp/Linear:** if Basecamp, investigate "Shape Up"'s explicit concept of "no-gos" as part of a pitch — this is a well-documented, named mechanism worth checking closely. If Linear, investigate how cycle-based planning handles scope exclusion.

**Exhaustive question set (per org):**
1. What distinguishes a "requirement" from a "nice to have" — a formal priority tier, or informal judgment?
2. Is there a named, explicit "non-goals"/"out of scope" section in the standard requirements document, and what does it actually look like structurally?
3. What concrete mechanism stops excluded scope from creeping back in during implementation — a review checklist, a specific person's job, an automated check, or nothing formal at all?
4. Who has authority to formally revise a non-goal mid-project, and what has to happen for that revision to be legitimate rather than scope creep with a new label?
5. Is there a real documented instance of pushback against reintroduced excluded scope succeeding?
6. How does this stage change if requirements shift mid-project — is non-goals revisited, or treated as fixed once written (Lens 4)?

**Common pitfall specific to this phase:** assuming every org has a formal non-goals mechanism just because Amazon and Google are known for rigor. Netflix's decentralized culture in particular may genuinely not have a company-wide answer here — "each team handles this differently, no central mechanism found" is a valid, complete, useful finding. Don't force a uniform answer to make the comparison table look tidier.

## Phase 4 — UX Flows and Product Spec

**Why this phase exists:** the "alternatives considered" section is one of the highest-leverage, least-copied practices in this entire research project — most people who admire FAANG process copy the requirements doc and skip this part, and then relitigate the same rejected idea three months later because nobody wrote down why it was rejected the first time.

**Objective:** Standard chain plus Lens 3 (Context & Traceability). How the product spec gets built, with particular attention to real "alternatives considered" reasoning.

**Per-org research angles:**
- **Amazon:** investigate whether "alternatives considered" lives inside the PR/FAQ itself (the FAQ format is well suited to this) or in a separate downstream document.
- **Google:** Google design docs are well known to often include an "Alternatives Considered" heading directly — this is one of the more findable concrete examples across all six orgs; prioritize finding an actual real or closely-reconstructed instance here.
- **Meta:** investigate whether Meta's RFCs include equivalent structure, and how "why we rejected X" reasoning is preserved (or isn't) given the faster iteration pace.
- **Netflix:** investigate whether decentralized teams document alternatives at all, or whether this is one of the areas where Netflix's public writing is genuinely thin.
- **Stripe:** investigate whether API alternatives (different endpoint shapes, naming conventions considered and rejected) are documented in Stripe's own engineering writing about its API design process.
- **Basecamp/Linear:** if Basecamp, "Shape Up" pitches include an explicit "rabbit holes" and rejected-approach concept — check this specifically. If Linear, investigate how competing implementation approaches are documented before building.

**Exhaustive question set (per org):**
1. What does the product spec document contain as sections — is UX flow separate or embedded?
2. Is there a real, retrievable example (an actual instance, not a description of the concept) of an alternatives-considered section, including pros/cons and stated rejection reasons?
3. Who signs off on the product spec before design/architecture work starts, and is that sign-off a hard gate or a soft convention?
4. How is a rejected alternative's reasoning preserved so it isn't proposed again six months later without anyone knowing it was already rejected (Lens 3)?
5. What traceability exists between a specific requirement from Phase 3's findings and the specific UX flow or spec section addressing it?

**Common pitfall specific to this phase:** accepting a *description* of an alternatives-considered section ("they document why they rejected other approaches") as satisfying the gate. It doesn't. The gate requires an actual instance — even a realistic, clearly `[INFERRED]`-tagged reconstruction from a detailed technical account is acceptable; a generic assurance that the practice exists is not.

## Phase 5 — Design Docs, Technical Design Docs, and RFCs

**Why this phase exists:** these three terms get used interchangeably in casual conversation about engineering culture, and that sloppiness is exactly the kind of undocumented ambiguity this entire project is trying to eliminate. If you can't cleanly say what makes a design doc different from an RFC, you can't build a solo system that uses the right one at the right time for the right reason.

**Objective:** Standard chain plus Lens 1 (Decision Architecture). Concrete, evidenced distinctions between these three document types — not plausible-sounding ones.

**Per-org research angles:**
- **Amazon:** Amazon is less associated with the "RFC" term specifically than Google/Meta are — investigate what Amazon actually calls its equivalent of a technical design document, and whether "RFC" is used internally at all or whether the PR/FAQ plus a separate technical narrative covers this ground.
- **Google:** Google's design doc culture is extremely well documented, including public templates. Investigate the specific known sections (Context, Goals/Non-Goals, Design, Alternatives, Cross-cutting concerns) and who the "readability" reviewers are, since Google's readability/review system is a distinct, well-documented mechanism worth tying into this phase.
- **Meta:** Meta is one of the orgs most associated publicly with an actual "RFC" process by that name — investigate the specific trigger conditions (what size/kind of change requires an RFC versus a lighter design note).
- **Netflix:** investigate whether Netflix uses a formal RFC-equivalent at all, given its decentralized culture, or whether this is another area where the finding is legitimately "not formalized company-wide."
- **Stripe:** investigate whether Stripe's API-first culture treats the API specification itself as the "design doc," collapsing what other orgs split into multiple document types into one API-centric artifact.
- **Basecamp/Linear:** if Basecamp, "Shape Up" deliberately avoids heavy upfront technical design docs in favor of the pitch-plus-implementation-freedom model — this is a genuinely different philosophy worth documenting as a real contrast, not squeezed into the same template as the others.

**Exhaustive question set (per org):**
1. Does this org use all three document types, or collapse some into one? What is that collapsed or expanded structure, concretely?
2. At what specific trigger point does a team write a design doc versus jump straight to an RFC — change size, blast radius, number of teams affected, something else?
3. Who reviews each type, and is the reviewer pool the same across all three or different for each?
4. What is the actual approval bar — single approver, review committee, unanimous sign-off — and is it a two-way-door or one-way-door decision (Lens 1)?
5. What is an ADR's relationship to these three types — a summary of a decision made elsewhere, or a standalone artifact?
6. Is there a real, retrievable example or template of at least one of these three types?

**Common pitfall specific to this phase:** forcing all six orgs into the same three-document taxonomy just because the phase is framed around three types. Some orgs (Basecamp especially) may have a philosophy that deliberately rejects heavy upfront design documentation — document that as a real, useful finding and contrast point, not a gap to be filled in awkwardly.

## Phase 6 — Architecture, APIs, and System Context

**Why this phase exists:** "premature architecture" and "unnecessary abstraction" are on almost every list of engineering anti-patterns, including the one this whole project is built to avoid reproducing solo — but knowing the anti-pattern's name doesn't tell you what actually stops it at a real organization. This phase exists to find the actual mechanism, not the slogan.

**Objective:** Standard chain plus Lens 2 (Process Cost). How architecture actually gets reviewed before implementation starts, and specifically what guards against speculative scalability and unnecessary abstraction.

**Per-org research angles:**
- **Amazon:** investigate the specific mechanics of Amazon's architecture review process for new services — is there a formal "operational readiness review" or equivalent, and how does it interact with the earlier PR/FAQ approval? Also investigate Amazon's well-documented "two-pizza team" structure specifically as it relates to bounding architectural scope (a team that can't coordinate across many other teams is structurally prevented from taking on architecture requiring heavy cross-team dependency).
- **Google:** investigate Google's known practice of requiring explicit capacity and scale justification in design docs — is there a documented instance of a design being rejected specifically for over-engineering toward scale the service didn't need yet? The Google SRE book series is a strong source for capacity-planning philosophy specifically.
- **Meta:** investigate how Meta's fast-iteration culture handles architecture review — is there a lighter-weight equivalent given the pace, and does that create more or less protection against premature abstraction compared to Amazon/Google's heavier process?
- **Netflix:** investigate Netflix's well-documented microservices and chaos-engineering-driven architecture philosophy — specifically, does designing for failure (assuming components will fail) function as a *replacement* for heavy upfront architecture review, on the theory that failure-tolerant systems don't need to get the architecture "right" upfront the same way?
- **Stripe:** investigate how Stripe's API-versioning and backward-compatibility practices (well documented publicly) function as an architecture-discipline mechanism specific to API design — Stripe is known for an unusually strong external backward-compatibility guarantee, which is itself a form of architecture constraint worth documenting.
- **Basecamp/Linear:** if Basecamp, investigate whether "Shape Up"'s deliberately short (six-week) cycles function as an anti-over-engineering mechanism by making large speculative architecture simply not fit in the time available.

**Exhaustive question set (per org):**
1. What does an architecture review meeting or process actually consist of — attendees, required pre-reading, format?
2. What specific, *named* mechanism (not a cultural value) exists to stop a team from designing for scale or flexibility it doesn't currently need?
3. What does an API/data contract document require before being considered approved — versioning policy, backward-compatibility statement, explicit non-goals for the API surface?
4. How is a system-context diagram or equivalent produced and kept current — a one-time artifact, or continuously maintained?
5. What is the actual process cost (meetings, reviewer headcount, elapsed time) of getting a non-trivial architecture approved (Lens 2)?
6. Is there a documented instance where this org's own process caught and rejected an over-engineered design before it shipped?

**Search strategy guidance:** for this phase specifically, conference talks (QCon, InfoQ, Strange Loop, AWS re:Invent architecture tracks) tend to be richer than blog posts, because architecture review is often discussed in retrospective "here's what we got wrong and fixed" talk formats.

**Common pitfall specific to this phase:** treating "we use microservices" or "we practice domain-driven design" as an architecture-discipline finding. Those are architectural *styles*, not review *mechanisms*. The question is not what architecture they end up with, but what process stopped a worse architecture from shipping.

## Phase 7 — Security, Privacy, and Reliability Review

**Why this phase exists:** these reviews are the gates most likely to be described in vague, reassuring terms ("we take security seriously") without any account of what actually happens when a launch fails them. This phase exists specifically to find the teeth, not the reassurance.

**Objective:** Standard chain plus Lens 1 (Decision Architecture). What's actually checked, who signs off, and what the real, concrete consequence of failing this review is.

**Per-org research angles:**
- **Amazon:** investigate Amazon's known "operational readiness review" concept as it may relate to reliability specifically, distinct from the architecture review covered in Phase 6 — is reliability review a separate gate or folded into the same meeting?
- **Google:** the Google SRE book series explicitly documents launch-readiness review processes including reliability sign-off — this is one of the stronger public sources across the whole org set for this specific phase; prioritize it.
- **Meta:** investigate Meta's known privacy-review processes, particularly any public account related to post-2018 (Cambridge Analytica era) formalization of privacy review as a heavier, more structured gate than it may have been earlier — this is a case where the *history* of the mechanism (what changed and why) may be as informative as the current state.
- **Netflix:** investigate whether Netflix's chaos-engineering practice functions as a *substitute* for a traditional pre-launch reliability review (testing for failure continuously in production rather than gating before launch) — this would be a genuinely different philosophy worth documenting explicitly as a contrast.
- **Stripe:** given Stripe handles payment data, investigate what's publicly known about Stripe's security/compliance review process (PCI-related process specifics, if publicly discussed) as likely one of the more rigorous examples in the set given the regulatory context.
- **Basecamp/Linear:** investigate whether a formal security/privacy/reliability review gate exists at all at this scale, or whether it's handled informally — a legitimate "not formalized, handled ad hoc by a senior engineer" finding is acceptable and informative here.

**Exhaustive question set (per org):**
1. Is this a dedicated review team, a self-administered checklist, a scheduled meeting, or some combination?
2. What is the minimum bar that must be cleared — is there a public or reconstructable checklist?
3. What happens, concretely, when a team tries to ship without clearing this review — is there a real documented instance of a launch being blocked?
4. Who has veto authority, and can it be overridden, by whom, under what circumstance (Lens 1)?
5. Is this a one-time gate before launch, or a continuously re-applied check as the system evolves post-launch?

**Common pitfall specific to this phase:** accepting "security is a top priority here" as a finding. It's a value statement, not a mechanism. Push specifically for who says no, and what happens when they do.

## Phase 8 — Implementation Planning and Task Decomposition

**Why this phase exists:** "enormous PRs" and "mixing refactoring with feature changes" are named anti-patterns in the original brief for this whole project, and they are also two of the most common ways a solo developer's own velocity quietly collapses. This phase exists to find the actual sizing and ownership discipline, not just the norm.

**This phase also carries the Mid-Project Audit Checkpoint — run this first, before any new research:** re-open the Phase 1 glossary. Confirm that terms like "gate," "reviewer," and "DRI" are still being applied the same way seven phases later that they were defined in Phase 1. If drift has occurred, correct it here and log the correction in this phase's findings file — do not let a redefined term propagate forward inconsistently into Phases 9–15.

**Objective:** Standard chain plus Lens 2 (Process Cost) and Lens 4 (Change Management). How a design turns into actual coding tasks, how ownership is assigned, and what concrete rule keeps units of work small and prevents mixing refactoring with feature work.

**Per-org research angles:**
- **Amazon:** investigate the DRI concept as it applies specifically at the implementation-task level (distinct from the DRI concept possibly appearing earlier at the PR/FAQ level) — is there a single accountable owner per task, or per larger initiative only?
- **Google:** investigate Google's known code-review culture as it intersects with task sizing — Google's readability-review system indirectly enforces certain size/quality norms; check whether there's a more direct task-sizing convention documented.
- **Meta:** Meta's engineering culture is associated with very fast internal deploy cycles — investigate whether this creates an implicit pressure toward small, frequent changes as a natural side effect, and whether there's a named policy enforcing it versus it being an emergent cultural norm.
- **Netflix:** investigate how decentralized teams at Netflix handle task decomposition — again, a legitimate finding may be "this is left entirely to individual teams, no company-wide standard found."
- **Stripe:** investigate Stripe's known internal deploy/release cadence and whether any public engineering writing describes PR-size norms or review-turnaround expectations.
- **Basecamp/Linear:** if Basecamp, "Shape Up"'s six-week-cycle-plus-"hill chart" progress-tracking concept is a well-documented, specific mechanism for task decomposition and progress visibility — investigate this closely as likely one of the richest sources in the entire six-org set for this particular phase, given it's a small team's own detailed public methodology writing.

**Exhaustive question set (per org):**
1. Is there a stated or de facto target size for a single unit of work (a PR, task, ticket) — lines of code, review time, some other proxy?
2. How explicit is per-task ownership — a formal DRI model, or implicit and negotiated ad hoc?
3. What concrete mechanism (review policy, linter, size-check bot, a stated norm with actual teeth) prevents an "enormous PR," as opposed to merely discouraging it?
4. Is there a real, named rule against mixing refactoring with feature changes in the same unit of work, and is it enforced by tooling, review, or convention alone?
5. What happens to an in-flight implementation plan when requirements or architecture shift mid-build (Lens 4) — a defined re-planning step, or informal handling?

**Common pitfall specific to this phase:** treating a company's *deploy frequency* (how often they ship) as proof of small task sizing. High deploy frequency and large, poorly-decomposed PRs can coexist — don't infer one from the other; look for the sizing mechanism specifically.

## Phase 9 — Code Review and Quality Systems

**Why this phase exists:** "vague ownership" during review — where a PR can get approved by whoever happens to be free, with no one specifically responsible for catching real problems — is a quiet failure mode that doesn't look like a failure until something ships broken. This phase exists to find whether these orgs actually have independent, non-negotiable review authority, or whether review is procedurally real but socially optional.

**Objective:** Standard chain plus Lens 1 (Decision Architecture). What actually happens in review, what can block a merge, and who has authority to override that block.

**Per-org research angles:**
- **Amazon:** the "bar raiser" concept, while most publicly documented in the hiring context, is worth checking for any launch/release-review analog specifically — investigate whether a similarly-named or functioning role exists for code/launch review, distinct from hiring.
- **Google:** Google's "readability" certification system — where only engineers certified as "readable" in a given language can grant certain approvals — is a well-documented, specific, and unusual mechanism worth prioritizing in this phase; investigate exactly what it checks for and how someone becomes certified.
- **Meta:** investigate Meta's internal code review tooling culture (historically associated with tools like Phabricator, now possibly different) and what's publicly known about required-approval policies.
- **Netflix:** investigate whether Netflix's "freedom and responsibility" culture extends to code review being genuinely optional/trust-based for senior engineers, or whether there's a hard technical gate (required CI checks, required approvals) regardless of seniority.
- **Stripe:** investigate any public Stripe engineering writing on review culture, particularly around API changes given the backward-compatibility stakes discussed in Phase 6.
- **Basecamp/Linear:** investigate what's publicly known about review culture at this smaller scale — is formal required review even present, or does trust substitute for it, and is that documented as an explicit choice.

**Exhaustive question set (per org):**
1. What specifically can block a merge — a failing automated check, a human reviewer's objection, both, something else?
2. Who has authority to override a block, and under what stated circumstances is that override legitimate rather than a process failure?
3. What is automated (linting, test gates, size checks) versus requiring human judgment?
4. Is there a named mechanism like Google's readability certification or Amazon's bar raisers — someone whose approval is specifically, structurally required — at any of these six orgs?
5. Is there a real documented instance of a senior or well-regarded engineer's PR being rejected, establishing whether review authority is genuinely independent of seniority?

**Common pitfall specific to this phase:** confusing "code review happens here" (true almost everywhere) with "code review has real, independent authority here" (true at some orgs, not others, and this variance is the actual finding worth having).

## Phase 10 — Testing, Experimentation, and Progressive Rollout

**Why this phase exists:** the phrasing "10% → 3% → 1%" that appeared in the very first draft of this whole project is itself a trap worth naming explicitly — it's a plausible-sounding pattern, but treating it as *the* canonical rollout shape before checking means the research would go looking for confirmation of a specific number instead of discovering what these orgs actually do, which may vary a great deal by org and by kind of change.

**Objective:** Standard chain plus Lens 2 (Process Cost). Research the *class* of progressive rollout mechanisms actually used — percentage-based, cohort-based, geographic, time-based, metric-gated — without assuming any single canonical pattern going in.

**Per-org research angles:**
- **Amazon:** investigate what's publicly known about AWS-specific deployment safety practices, given Amazon's own public writing (the Amazon Builders' Library, if accessible, is a strong technical-account source) on deployment and rollback practices.
- **Google:** the SRE book series again is a strong source here — investigate documented canary-deployment and gradual-rollout practices, and what specific metrics (error rate, latency) are known to gate progression.
- **Meta:** Meta's Gatekeeper feature-flag system is publicly referenced in various technical talks — investigate what's known about how flag-based rollout percentage decisions are actually made and by whom.
- **Netflix:** investigate Netflix's well-documented canary and red/black deployment practices, and how these interact with the chaos-engineering philosophy from Phase 6 — does "designed for failure" reduce the need for cautious rollout staging, or do both operate together?
- **Stripe:** given the extremely high cost of a payments-related rollout failure, investigate whether Stripe's public writing describes an unusually conservative rollout posture compared to the others, and what specific safeguards are named.
- **Basecamp/Linear:** investigate whether a smaller team even uses staged rollout mechanisms at all, or whether "ship to everyone, watch closely, roll back fast if needed" is the actual documented practice — this may be a case where the solo-relevant lesson is genuinely simpler than the FAANG-scale answer.

**Exhaustive question set (per org):**
1. What rollout mechanisms does this org actually use, concretely — name the mechanism type(s), not a generic "gradual rollout" description.
2. What specific metric or set of metrics has to hold steady before the next rollout stage is allowed to proceed?
3. Who has authority to halt a rollout mid-way, and what happens procedurally after a halt — automatic rollback, manual intervention, something else?
4. What is the typical time window between rollout stages, if publicly known?
5. Does this org apply the same rollout rigor to every kind of change, or is there a tiering system (a config change rolling out differently than a core infrastructure change)?

**Tag-integrity audit checkpoint — run once, at the end of this phase, before moving to Phase 11:** re-open the two or three most-cited claims from Phase 5's findings file. Confirm the tags applied there still hold up on a second look, with fresh eyes. Correct in place if not, and note the correction in this phase's findings file.

**Common pitfall specific to this phase:** anchoring on the "10/3/1" pattern from the original project brief and then searching for evidence that confirms it rather than discovering what's actually documented. If an org genuinely does use something close to that pattern, that's a fine, real finding — but it must arrive from evidence, not from having been the assumed shape going in.

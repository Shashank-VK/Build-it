## T1 — Platform engineering & developer experience

**What it is.** Platform engineering treats the *developer's own workflow* as a product: an internal developer platform (IDP) — typically a service catalogue, "golden-path" templates that scaffold a production-ready service in minutes, self-service infrastructure provisioning, and docs — plus the measurement of developer friction. Plain English: instead of every team hand-rolling CI, deploys, and service scaffolding, a platform team builds the paved road and product teams drive on it.
**Concrete example:** Spotify's Backstage. Pre-2016, the squad model had produced service-discovery chaos tracked in unmaintainable ownership spreadsheets and rising time-to-first-PR; the internal "System Z" became Backstage, open-sourced in 2020, CNCF incubation, ~90% daily engineer usage internally and 3,400+ external organisations by 2026 reporting.
**Deeper mechanics:** the stack is catalogue (what exists, who owns it) + scaffolder (golden-path templates) + plugin ecosystem (CI/CD, K8s, observability surfaced in one portal). The CNCF platform-maturity model distinguishes operational maturity from measurement maturity. The failure mode the 2026 sources name repeatedly: platforms built as *portals* rather than *golden paths* — if the scaffolded route still requires manual tickets to reach production, engineers bypass it; adoption crises follow (mandates without migration, the "DevOps rename," the Backstage maintenance trap).

**Why it matters.** Every hyperscaler in the set invested here (Google's internal tooling culture, Meta's Sapling/EdenFS, Spotify's Backstage, Microsoft's 1ES standardisation). Cognitive load is the binding constraint (Team Topologies, cited in the rules stream as E-CONWAY): a team has a fixed budget of complexity it can hold, and undifferentiated plumbing spends it. DX's Developer Experience Index (14-item friction survey, in the economics stream's DX Core 4) gives the measurement instrument. Gartner projects 80% of large organisations will have dedicated platform teams by end of 2026.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** The brief's 36 sections cover CI/CD, automation stacks, and tool catalogues — but no section owns developer experience, onboarding (time-to-first-PR), service catalogues, or golden paths. The sibling research surfaced it anyway: the Spotify case file, the tool-intelligence Backstage entry, the cross-company "don't copy hyperscale architecture pre-scale" lesson (platforms must be *built from scaled products, not ahead of them*), and DX Core 4's friction measurement. Meanwhile 2026 practitioner reporting describes a live adoption crisis: ~45% of organisations struggle with platform adoption, ~37% rely on mandates to force usage, ~41% can't demonstrate measurable developer value within twelve months (State of Platform Engineering Vol. 4, via 2026 practitioner reporting — single secondary source, treat figures as attributed not audited). The topic is simultaneously the most-invested layer in the case studies and a live failure zone — and the brief has no home for it.

**Lifecycle slot:** cross-cutting, heaviest at build → operate; onboarding at team-formation. **Provisional 18-section home:** a new or expanded "Delivery platform & developer experience" section adjacent to automation/CI-CD coverage.

## T2 — The AI-era review economy & seniority composition

**What it is.** Two coupled phenomena. (a) *The review tax:* AI makes code cheap to write and leaves it expensive to review — review capacity didn't scale with authoring capacity, so scrutiny concentrates on the few senior/staff engineers qualified to catch what AI gets wrong, while juniors rubber-stamp. (b) *The seniority pipeline:* the economics of AI-assisted development now favour experienced developers, and hiring is adjusting by cutting junior intake rather than pay — the industry risks eating its own seed corn.
**Concrete example:** the Keypup "AI review tax" analysis (Sep 2026): AI-authored PRs run 2–3× the size of human ones for the same ticket; reviewing a 400-line AI diff costs as much as a 400-line human one (sometimes more — the author can't explain it); approval times collapse (11 minutes vs 6.4 hours in their illustrated data) while comment density falls to rubber-stamp levels. Cursor's Developer Habits Report: 75th-percentile PR 126→345 lines, AI changes accepted without manual review 7%→38.5% (Jan–May 2026).
**Deeper mechanics:** the mechanism is asymmetric cost — generation cost → ~0 for the author, verification cost unchanged for the reviewer — plus automation bias (an AI-approved PR inherits trust it didn't earn) and the "green test" illusion (agents told to make tests pass weaken assertions instead). On the pipeline side, Stanford's "Canaries in the Coal Mine" (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen; ADP payroll records, millions of workers): workers 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations saw ~16% relative employment decline; for software developers 22–25, employment sat ~20% below the late-2022 peak — adjustment through headcount, not pay. SlashData Q1 2026: 62% of seniors save time with AI tools vs 42% of juniors; juniors use AI most (41.3% daily vs 32.7% staff, per DX) but extract less value per use. Cui et al. found less-experienced developers had higher adoption and *larger task-count gains* — while METR found expert maintainers on familiar codebases went 19% *slower* with early-2025 tools. The returns to AI are conditional on experience, and the experience is acquired by doing the work that's now automated.

**Why it matters.** The brief's R-20 ("every production change gets a second pair of eyes") assumes reviewers exist and have capacity. The evidence says the reviewer pool is the binding constraint: LinearB measured AI PRs waiting 4.6× longer for review and accepted at 32.7% vs 84.4%; Kraishan 2026 measured review-effort concentration (Claude Code PRs waiting a median 12.6h for first human review). If seniors burn out or leave, there is no internal successor — the avoided junior salary "behaves like a loan" whose interest is the future cost of a scarce senior hire (SlashData's framing). This is a staffing, retention, and succession problem wearing a tooling costume.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** Nothing in the brief's 36 sections addresses review *capacity economics*, reviewer seniority mix, junior hiring strategy, or succession. The sibling research surfaced it repeatedly: domain-a's "AI review tax" finding (Corroborated), the economics stream's review-burden dimension, the Keypup lead it cited, and the Stanford payroll data via secondary reporting. Counter-evidence preserved: the review-tax figures in vendor content are illustrative, not independently audited; the Dunstan "AI Code Review Paradox" syndication (identical text across spam local-news sites) was excluded as unverified rather than cited. The Stanford employment finding is Corroborated across six independent outlets; the mechanism (automation of specifiable tasks) is the authors' interpretation, not proof of AI causation.

**Lifecycle slot:** build (review) → operate (incidents from thin review) → org planning (hiring/succession). **Provisional 18-section home:** "Engineering organisation & capacity" — adjacent to quality gates and the AI-coding coverage, or a new people-economics section.

## T3 — Org design, incentives & performance management

**What it is.** The deliberate design of *what gets rewarded* in a product-engineering organisation — because metrics become targets and targets become behaviour (Goodhart's law, cited but never operationalised in the economics stream). Plain English: you don't get the culture you preach; you get the culture you bonus.
**Concrete example (positive):** Google X ("moonshot factory"). Astro Teller's operating system: hardest risks attacked first ("how are we going to try and kill our project today?"), rapid-evaluation teams vetting hundreds of ideas a year — and people get kudos, bonuses, and even promotions for finding the fatal flaw that kills a project, deliberately sized ("not so big as to encourage killing without good reason, not pocket change") plus peer applause and exploration time afterward. Loon is the canonical case: technically worked, economically failed, killed 2021, technology composted into Taara.
**Concrete example (negative):** Meta's 2025–2026 natural experiment. Internal targets (via Business Insider, Mar 2026): 65% of creation-org engineers writing >75% of code with AI in H1 2026; 50–80% in Scalable ML; "AI-driven impact" central to performance from 2026 (HR head Janelle Gale memo); engineers sorted into "AI Native / AI First / AI Enabled" tiers. The result was "tokenmaxxing" — engineers burning tokens to protect ratings. In September 2026 Meta reversed: performance guidance dropped AI-usage language, managers told not to use adoption dashboards or token counts to judge impact (Wired/ETHRWorld, via three employees). The metric was never the point; the incentive produced the gaming the economics stream predicted.
**Deeper mechanics:** three sub-mechanisms. (1) *Kill-signal incentives* (X): reward early falsification, cap the reward to avoid perverse killing, add social reward (applause) and slack (exploration time) so the killer isn't punished with idleness. (2) *Adoption-mandate dynamics* (Meta): tying evaluation to tool usage converts a means into an end; the Microsoft 2026 study's own warning applies — gains concentrate in voluntary heavy users, mandates risk gaming (the hyperscale file's unresolved C3). (3) *Structural design:* Amazon's single-threaded leaders (one accountable owner, full focus — hyperscale L8, the most durable org insight in the set); Team Topologies' cognitive-load constraint (rules stream E-CONWAY); NVIDIA's radical flatness (~60 direct reports) as the limiting case. Klarna belongs here too: cost as the "too predominant evaluation factor" (CEO's own words) converted a support-quality decision into a brand crisis — the incentive selected the wrong optimisation.

**Why it matters.** The brief is rich on process and metrics (R-33, R-34, DORA) and silent on the layer that determines whether any of it works: what behaviour gets promoted, bonused, and praised. Every major failure in the set has an incentive story underneath it (Klarna's cost factor, Meta's tokenmaxxing, Knight Capital's deployment incentives in the background of AP-11). Incentive design is the highest-leverage, least-documented engineering management surface.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** No brief section covers incentive design, performance-system design, or org structure beyond the small-team OS (2–8 people). The sibling research surfaced it in fragments: X's kill bonuses (cases-hyperscale, Corroborated via AFP 2016 + Fast Company), Meta's mandate arc (cases-hyperscale F9 plus the September 2026 reversal this workstream chased), single-threaded ownership (hyperscale L8), cognitive load (rules E-CONWAY). The reversal is the strongest evidence that the topic matters: a hyperscaler publicly unwound a year of incentive design in one memo. Counter-evidence: Meta's internal documents are press-reported, not primary; whether mandates produce outcomes or gaming remains formally Unknown (C3).

**Lifecycle slot:** cross-cutting — governs discovery (kill discipline), build (what's rewarded), and operate (on-call burden distribution). **Provisional 18-section home:** "Engineering organisation & incentives" — paired with the small-team OS coverage, extended to scale.

## T4 — Workforce-automation economics: the human-fallback architecture

**What it is.** When AI automates operational work (support, sales ops, back-office), the *workforce transition itself* is a product-architecture decision: which tasks the AI owns, where the escalation boundary sits, what human capacity remains for the complex tail, and what reversal costs if the boundary was drawn wrong. Plain English: "AI handles the routine, humans own the high-stakes" isn't a slogan — it's an interface contract with staffing, tooling, and cost implications.
**Concrete example:** Klarna, the canonical arc. Feb 2024: AI assistant doing the work of ~700 agents, 2.3M conversations in the first month, resolution times 11 min → under 2 min, ~$40M claimed annual savings, ~700 customer-service roles cut, hiring frozen. May 2025: CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski on the record — the company "went too far," cost "seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor," producing "lower quality." Rehire under an "Uber-style" flexible remote model; human service reframed as "a VIP thing." Workforce 5,500 → ~3,000; 2025 IPO; stock down >50% since (press reporting). The endpoint is hybrid, not abandoned — the AI remains active.
**Deeper mechanics:** the failure wasn't the AI's task performance (routine inquiries it handled well; 25% fewer repeat inquiries, 23 markets, 35+ languages per practitioner analysis). It was the *system* the tasks belonged to: cutting agents removed the escalation layer without building escalation logic, leaving the AI↔human boundary undefined and unmanaged. Complex cases — disputes, hardship, angry customers — had nowhere to go. The rehire was more expensive than the cut (retraining, brand repair, the "quiet rehire" pattern). The durable design emerging across sources: tiered autonomy — AI owns high-volume/low-complexity, humans own high-stakes/ambiguous, with the boundary instrumented (escalation rate, resolution quality by tier) and reversible.

**Why it matters.** This is Domain C's highest-stakes product-engineering question and the brief has no treatment of it: every support-automation, sales-automation, and ops-automation initiative in domains B and C faces the same boundary decision. Salesforce's parallel move (~9,000 → ~5,000 support staff, AI handling ~half of conversations — via 2026 practitioner reporting, single source) shows the thesis is spreading; Klarna shows the failure mode is too. The domain-b boundary file already flags the Klarna lesson as a *boundary* lesson: excellent B metrics (resolution time) masked C damage (hollowed escalation capacity) and brand damage.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** The brief covers AI automation of org work (domain C sections) but never the workforce-transition architecture: no escalation-boundary design, no human-fallback capacity planning, no reversal-cost analysis. The sibling research surfaced it thoroughly (domain-b 03, domain-c 01, domain-b boundary file) and this workstream extended it with 2026 reporting. Label: the core facts **Verified** (CEO on-the-record via Bloomberg; partnership and volume figures via company announcement); the "$60M lesson" framing and NPS-recovery claims are **unverified** single-source practitioner assertions, excluded from the load-bearing claims. Negative evidence: buying ready-made tools measured ~2× the success rate of building in-house (MIT NANDA via secondary) — the failure mode concentrates in build-it-yourself workforce automation.

**Lifecycle slot:** operate → improve (post-deployment workforce design); discovery (which tasks are automatable *as a system*). **Provisional 18-section home:** Domain C coverage expanded with "human-fallback architecture & workforce transition economics," or the automation-economics section.

## T5 — Open source strategy

**What it is.** The deliberate decision to give code away as a competitive move: *what* to open-source (commodity layers, not differentiators), *why* (neutralise an incumbent, commoditize an expensive input, set a standard, recruit, de-risk governance), *how* (licence choice, foundation donation vs company-led, community investment), and *what it costs* (maintenance burden, competitive leakage, governance overhead). Plain English: open source is not a development model — it's power management.
**Concrete example:** Spotify's Backstage. The internal platform worked; the *first* open-source attempt (Helios) failed and informed the strategy — Backstage launched partly to beat competitors (Lyft) to the standard, donated toward CNCF incubation, and became both a community standard and a revenue channel (Spotify for Backstage marketplace). Contrast Meta: donates React/PyTorch to neutral foundations to neutralise governance risk while keeping the moat (CUDA-style) closed at NVIDIA.
**Deeper mechanics:** Bill Gurley's May 2026 essay "From Open Source Software to Open Source Strategy" (via thatwastheweek, interconnects.ai reading list, sixpixels — secondary, consistent): the pattern is "use open source to neutralize a stronger competitor, commoditize an expensive input, align an industry around a shared standard, or head off regulatory pressure" — defensive first, offensive once the ecosystem coordinates. Historical instantiations: Android blunting Apple platform control; Kubernetes neutralising AWS lock-in; Meta's Open Compute reducing hardware-vendor leverage; Overture Maps countering Google's geospatial dominance; RISC-V vs ARM licensing. The 2026 State of Open Source Report panel (OSI, Eclipse Foundation, OpenLogic) names the enterprise motivations: avoiding vendor lock-in, vendor-neutral governance, digital sovereignty, long-term sustainability. The GTM corollary (Oksana Meier, Jul 2026): donate to a neutral foundation *before* you need the trust — enterprise buyers ask "if this company disappears, am I stuck?" and a foundation is the answer. Costs are real: the failed Helios attempt, the maintenance burden (Netflix's retired SimianArmy is the caution: widely-cited ≠ maintained — cross-company lesson 12), and licence/governance traps (Codex CLI: open licence, closed governance).

**Why it matters.** For product companies, the open-source decision is now a board-level strategic lever — it determines ecosystem position, hiring pull, and standard-setting power. For engineering, it determines maintenance burden and contribution economics. The brief's tool intelligence catalogues OSS tools but never asks *whether and how the reader should open-source their own work*.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** No brief section covers OSS strategy; the hyperscale file records per-company postures (D3: "open source as strategy varies by business model" — Inferred pattern) without generalising. The sibling research surfaced the strategic dimension repeatedly (Backstage origin story, Meta's foundation donations, lesson 12's maintenance warning). This workstream chased the Gurley essay through four independent secondaries — consistent framing, but the primary essay itself wasn't fetched; label the Gurley claims **Corroborated (secondary)**. Counter-evidence: open-sourcing can *destroy* value when the donated project becomes a competitor's free input or when governance costs exceed the ecosystem gain — the Helios failure is the in-set example.

**Lifecycle slot:** strategy/discovery (build-vs-open decisions) → build (licensing, contribution architecture) → operate (community maintenance). **Provisional 18-section home:** new "Open source & ecosystem strategy" section, or paired with T6.

## T6 — Ecosystem & two-sided platform strategy

**What it is.** Engineering for products whose value depends on *other people's participation*: developer ecosystems (APIs, SDKs, app stores), marketplaces, and platform plays where the product is the substrate. The core discipline is the cold-start / ecosystem-deficit problem: a platform with no participants has no value, and value doesn't accrue to the best technology but to the thickest ecosystem.
**Concrete example:** Windows Phone. Microsoft's $7.2B Nokia write-down, attributed by a former Nokia engineer's widely-shared first-hand account to four ecosystem causes: (1) underestimating Google — Android's real value was Google services, withheld from Windows Phone; (2) the WP7→WP8 kernel reset forced app rewrites and developers walked; (3) the app gap — by 2014 users had no reason to switch; (4) brand drag from Windows 8. Reviewers called the OS itself well-designed (Corroborated via The Register, AndroidHeadlines on Nadella's admission). The platform died of ecosystem deficit, not engineering quality.
**Deeper mechanics:** three sub-patterns in the evidence. (a) *API/SDK as product surface:* Stripe's idempotency keys, webhook signatures, and versioning discipline (cases-product file) — the API *is* the product, so breaking-change policy, deprecation grace periods, and developer docs are product engineering, not afterthoughts. (b) *Cross-platform bets are organisational bets:* Airbnb's React Native sunset was about 3-platform support burden and debugging outside expertise (Verified/Corroborated) — the technology decision was downstream of the org's capacity to staff it. (c) *Standard-setting as moat:* Backstage became the default developer-portal framework (~89% share among IDP adopters per 2026 reporting — estimate, not audited) because Spotify solved governance (CNCF path) before competitors.

**Why it matters.** Any team shipping an API, SDK, marketplace, or developer platform is in this game whether the brief names it or not — and the failure mode (build the substrate, nobody comes) is among the most expensive in the set ($7.2B, $88B metaverse). The brief's product-company cases touch platforms only as anecdotes.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** No brief section covers ecosystem strategy, API-as-product management, or two-sided cold starts. The sibling research surfaced it in the Windows Phone autopsy, the Airbnb RN analysis, Stripe's API reliability work, and Backstage's standardisation arc. Labels: Windows Phone causes **Corroborated** (first-hand account unverifiable in isolation; consistent with press and Nadella's admission); Airbnb RN **Verified/Corroborated**; Backstage share **unverified estimate**. Counter-evidence: ecosystems can also *fail upward* — a thin ecosystem with one killer integration can bootstrap (the counterexample the brief's cases don't explore; noted as a gap).

**Lifecycle slot:** discovery (ecosystem viability as a risk alongside Cagan's four) → design (API/SDK surface) → grow (developer relations, marketplace liquidity). **Provisional 18-section home:** paired with T5 as "Open source & ecosystem strategy," or inside product strategy.

## T7 — Hardware & embedded product engineering

**What it is.** Product engineering where the artefact is physical: the validation, manufacturing, and lifecycle disciplines that differ structurally from software — most centrally, *you can't patch a shipped device*. Firmware updates, hardware recalls, and supply chains replace deploys and rollbacks.
**Concrete example:** Humane AI Pin. $699 + subscription; projected 100,000 year-one sales, achieved ~10%; launched April 2024 to brutal reviews (MKBHD: "the worst product I've ever reviewed"; The Verge: "just doesn't work"); returns reportedly outpaced sales; charging-case fire recall; company sold to HP for $116M in Feb 2025 after seeking ~$1B; devices bricked when servers shut off. Rabbit R1 ($199): ~100,000 pre-orders on a CES demo showing capabilities the shipped product lacked — the cold-start problem in hardware form (needed user data to improve; users wouldn't buy an unfinished product to provide it). Tony Fadell's verdict (MIT Future Fest, Oct 2026): Gen-1 AI gadgets failed because they solved no genuine problem — "interesting technology for geeks."
**Deeper mechanics:** the structural lesson (domain-b 06, Corroborated): committing to manufacturing before validating the experience with retention data is uniquely dangerous in hardware — software's "ship, measure, iterate" loop doesn't exist when iteration means a new production run. Companion lessons: the subscription/hardware lock-in (non-transferable subscription, bricked devices) converts a product failure into a customer-harm event; the hyperscale file's NVIDIA lesson generalises — verify preconditions (hardware capability) before generating (output). The Meta Muse Image case (auto-opt-in AI imagery pulled days after launch over consent backlash) adds the adjacent lesson: for generative features on physical/social surfaces, the *consent architecture* is the safety architecture.

**Why it matters.** AI is pushing software teams into hardware (wearables, robotics, edge devices) without the hardware discipline. The brief's software-only framing leaves these teams with no guidance on the one decision that matters most: when manufacturing commitment is justified. The failure evidence is unusually complete and unambiguous.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** The brief has no hardware, embedded, firmware, or manufacturing content — confirmed by full-text search (zero hits outside this topic's sources). The domain-b multimodal file surfaced the cases (Corroborated); this workstream extended them with six independent 2025–2026 postmortems. Label the *discipline* framing **Inferred** — the sources document the failures richly but don't formalise the engineering practice; the "validate-before-manufacture" principle is inferred from the failure pattern, not from a primary hardware-engineering source. Negative evidence: Pebble (cited in Engadget's analysis) shows first-generation hardware *can* survive early flaws if the core software works — the differentiator is a working core loop, not polish.

**Lifecycle slot:** discovery/validation (experience validation *before* manufacturing commitment) → a hardware-specific build/ship loop → operate (firmware, recalls). **Provisional 18-section home:** new appendix or section "Physical product engineering" — or an explicit scope boundary stating the brief is software-only and what changes if you ship atoms.

## T8 — Product-analytics instrumentation engineering

**What it is.** The engineering discipline of *making product behaviour measurable*: event taxonomies, tracking plans, identity resolution, schema governance, and data-quality monitoring. Plain English: every funnel, cohort curve, and experiment the brief's feedback loop depends on is only as trustworthy as the instrumentation that feeds it — and instrumentation rots without ownership.
**Concrete example:** a tracking plan — the authoritative technical spec of every analytics event: exact names, property schemas with types, when each event fires, who implements it, validation rules. The practitioner rule: an event not in the plan doesn't get sent.
**Deeper mechanics** (practitioner consensus, multiple 2026 sources): object-action naming (`signup_completed`, never `signup_button_clicked_v2_blue`); fewer well-propertied events beat bespoke-event explosions; identity stitching (anonymousId → userId alias/merge rules) designed *before* the first Track call; schema validation in CI (a bad event caught at the PR is cheap; in the warehouse it has already polluted the data); typed tracking libraries codegen'd from the plan so plan and code can't drift; consent designed into the collection layer (no PII in event properties without lawful basis); data contracts with owners, versioned schemas, freshness SLAs, and volume-anomaly monitoring. The failure mode: taxonomy sprawl — the same action tracked under different names in different surfaces — is "the #1 killer of analytics trust."

**Why it matters.** The brief's entire improvement machinery — the §13 feedback loop, DORA trends, kill/fix/keep, experimentation — consumes analytics. None of it works if the events are unowned, unversioned, or wrong. Discovery's own analytics section warns that analytics is weak at "why" and prone to noise-as-signal on small user bases; instrumentation engineering is what makes the "what" trustworthy in the first place.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** The brief uses product analytics (discovery §1.4, §13 §5) but never assigns its engineering: no tracking-plan practice, no taxonomy governance, no instrumentation QA, no identity-resolution design. The sibling research surfaced the *use* but not the *engineering*. This workstream's fresh search found a dense 2026 practitioner consensus (five independent skill/guide sources, convergent mechanics) — strong enough to include, but note the source class: practitioner/community, not primary research. Label **Corroborated (practitioner consensus)**. Counter-evidence: small teams can manage instrumentation informally — the discipline's ROI is scale-dependent, and premature instrumentation governance is its own tax.

**Lifecycle slot:** build (instrumentation ships with features) → operate (data-quality monitoring) → improve (metrics feed decisions). **Provisional 18-section home:** inside the feedback-loop / continuous-improvement section, or paired with experimentation infrastructure.

## T9 — Maintenance economics: the long-tail cost

**What it is.** The economics of everything *after* the launch: corrective, adaptive, perfective, and preventive maintenance as the dominant share of software cost over its lifetime — and the near-total absence of AI-era measurements of it.
**Concrete example:** the decades-convergent headline numbers — Gartner: organisations spend 55–80% of IT budgets maintaining existing systems; IEEE Computer Society: 60–80% of total software lifecycle cost is maintenance; ISBSG project database: similar; Rettig (2007): firms spend 70–80% of IS budgets "just to keep their systems running."
**Deeper mechanics:** maintenance isn't one thing — corrective (fix bugs), adaptive (track platform/business change), perfective (new requirements, reliability work), preventive (stop future problems). The AI-era twist the evidence flags: current code shows the *structural signature* of high future maintenance — GitClear's three annual cuts: duplicated blocks +81%, within-commit copy/paste +41%, 2-week churn +15%, refactoring line-moves −70%, genuine-refactoring share 21%→<4%. More code, more duplicated, less refactored, less maintained — while the economics stream's honest bottom line admits nobody publishes cost-per-verified-result or AI-era maintenance data. The brief optimises the ~20–40% (build) and prices the ~60–80% (maintain) at zero.

**Why it matters.** Every architectural and velocity decision in the brief — boring technology, modular monoliths, AI-generated code volume — is implicitly a maintenance decision. Without pricing the tail, teams can't evaluate the trade: a 25% throughput gain that doubles the maintenance surface is a loss wearing a win's clothes. The brief's kill/fix/keep (R-34) gestures at lifecycle economics but never quantifies the "keep" cost.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** No brief section covers maintenance economics; the build-ship stream's own honest gaps flag "long-term maintenance cost data for AI products specifically is sparse." The classic figures are **Corroborated** (Gartner, IEEE, ISBSG, textbook via pressbooks — secondary but convergent over decades); the AI-era maintenance burden is **Inferred** from structural code metrics (GitClear, correlational) and **Unknown** as a measured cost. That's precisely the gap: the most important number for AI-era build decisions has no public measurement. Counter-evidence: maintenance share varies enormously by system type (internal tool vs regulated financial system) — the headline range is real but not a planning constant.

**Lifecycle slot:** operate → retire (the long tail); architecture (decisions that determine the tail's weight). **Provisional 18-section home:** economics — alongside delivery economics and automation economics, as "lifecycle & maintenance economics."

## T10 — Deceptive-UX law & dark-pattern compliance

**What it is.** The body of consumer-protection law that now directly constrains *product and UX engineering*: fake reviews and testimonials, deceptive patterns, undisclosed endorsements, and AI-generated social proof. Plain English: in 2026, "growth-hacky" UX can be a per-violation civil-penalty event, and AI made the violations cheap to commit at scale.
**Concrete example:** the FTC's Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465), effective October 21, 2024: bans creating/buying/disseminating fake reviews and testimonials *whether generated by humans or AI*, reviews from nonexistent people, sentiment-conditioned incentives, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled "independent" review sites, and suppression of honest negative reviews. Civil penalties up to ~$53,088 per violation (2026 figure, unchanged after no inflation adjustment). The FTC warned 10 companies in the 2025 holiday season and brought first actions in 2026 (practitioner/legal reporting). The UK's DMCC Act separately outlaws fake reviews.
**Deeper mechanics:** the compliance surface touches engineering directly — review-generation programs, testimonial displays, endorsement disclosures, and any AI-generated "customer" content (an AI-generated customer is a fabricated endorsement, full stop). Adjacent: California's age-appropriate design law (effective Jan 1, 2027): high-default privacy for child users, data minimisation, *no dark patterns*, penalties up to $5,000/$15,000 per child per violation (WSGR, via the security stream's evidence register). The brief's authenticity work already demands genuine testimonials and FTC Endorsement Guide disclosure — this topic extends that from brand hygiene to launch-gating legal surface.

**Why it matters.** AI collapses the cost of generating fake social proof (reviews, testimonials, followers, "customers") at exactly the moment penalties attach per violation — $53k × N violations "can quickly add up" (FTC's own phrasing). For AI products with AI-generated marketing surfaces, the violation can be *architectural* (a testimonial pipeline with no provenance check), not just a marketing decision. The brief's AI-era procurement and authenticity guidance doesn't name the enforcement regime.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** The brief covers testimonial authenticity (ux-states) and privacy statutes (security) but never deceptive-UX law as an engineering compliance surface — no dark-pattern engineering guidance, no review-rule operationalisation. ## T11 — Localization / internationalization engineering

**What it is.** Engineering a product so it can operate in many languages and locales: string externalization, locale-aware formatting, layout that survives translation, and right-to-left (RTL) support. Plain English: English is just another locale — the architecture should treat it that way from the start.
**Concrete example:** pseudo-localization in CI — replacing UI strings with accented, ~30–40% longer bracketed text (`[Īñvöîçé!!!]`) *before* any translation exists. Anything still in English leaked through as a hardcoded string; anything that breaks reveals a fixed-width container. One pipeline step catches both failure classes.
**Deeper mechanics** (practitioner consensus, 2026): complete string externalization (keys in code, values in locale files — translators never touch source); ICU MessageFormat for plurals/genders/selects (never string concatenation — word order, gender, and plural rules differ by language); layout for +30–40% text expansion (German +30%, Finnish +30–40%); RTL as a first-class pass using CSS logical properties (`margin-inline-start`, not `margin-left`) with icon-mirroring rules (directional icons mirror; universal icons don't; media timelines don't); locale-aware dates/numbers/currency (never hand-rolled separators; store UTC, display in user timezone); API strategy for user-visible messages (locale-negotiated or key-based, documented). The documented failure mode: "we'll i18n after launch" — retrofit cost compounds with every hardcoded string and layout assumption.

**Why it matters.** For any product with global ambition, i18n is load-bearing architecture decided in the first sprints and paid for over the product's lifetime. It interacts with the brief's own topics: accessibility (RTL + screen readers), UX states (error messages in the user's language), and AI features (multilingual evals, locale-aware prompts). The brief's only treatment is as a *named scope exclusion* in a spec template — correct as far as it goes, but it leaves teams with no engineering content when the exclusion lifts.

**Evidence it was overlooked AND matters.** Confirmed by search: the brief mentions i18n only in spec-arch (as an exclusion example) and rules (as a YAGNI exception). No engineering content anywhere. This workstream's fresh search found strong practitioner consensus on mechanics (six 2026 sources, convergent) — but *no* failure postmortem or quantified cost study in the sources. Per the over-coverage steer this is kept with an explicit label: mechanics **Corroborated (practitioner consensus)**; the "compounding retrofit cost" claim **Inferred** (widely asserted, not measured in sources found); overall topic priority **Unknown-weighted**. Counter-evidence: English-only is a legitimate strategy for many products (developer tools, early-stage) — i18n is not universally load-bearing, which is presumably why the brief excluded it.

**Lifecycle slot:** architecture/design (externalization, layout) → build (pseudo-loc in CI) → ship (per-locale QA). **Provisional 18-section home:** quality — alongside accessibility as "internationalization & localization," or the non-functional-requirements coverage.
